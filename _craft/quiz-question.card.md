# Card — quiz-question
Applies to every unit in the bank — one 1–2 sentence second-person situation plus exactly four terse declarative options — run before the unit is committed to `questions.ts`.

**Doctrine inverted 2026-08-27.** The bank used to ban all Star Wars content from the
questions ("zero fandom — mundane-but-loaded situations only, all the source material
lives in the results"). That made the quiz read as a generic personality test with a skin,
which the research finding behind this card explains: the Pottermore Sorting Hat is
75–80% world-neutral in its actual questions, and the magic is in the wrapper, not the
nouns. In-world questions are now the standard. See `question-lint.py`'s ALLOW / WARN /
DENY tiers for the mechanical enforcement; this card carries the judgment calls.

## Categories
Every unit lands in one of four. Only the last is the target.
- **Trivia** — has a correct answer outside the reader (a lore fact). Bad.
- **Referential** — no correct answer, but the reader can't form a preference without
  knowing what a named thing *is*. Bad.
- **Costumed** — a world-neutral dilemma with nouns found-and-replaced ("car" → "speeder").
  Worst of the four: it reads as fake, which is worse than reading as generic.
- **Diegetic** — set inside the world, every noun plain English or glossed in the clause
  it lives in, and the dilemma is generated by a rule that is only true in this world.
  This is the target.

## World-pressures
Every question should trace to one of these. They are the load-bearing facts of the
setting — deliver them through the situation, not through a name.
- an occupying authority can stop and search you, and paperwork is a weapon
- machines are people-ish, and how you treat one is characterising
- distance is fatal; nobody is coming to help
- there is an intuition some people trust and others sneer at
- being in debt to criminals is an ordinary way to live
- a working ship is the difference between freedom and being stuck
- wars are fought by ordinary people in old, failing equipment
- mentors and family are load-bearing and frequently go bad

## Tests
- Delete-the-sentence: remove any sentence of the prompt — does the remaining prompt still pose the same choice? If yes, that sentence advanced nothing; cut it.
- Downstream-state test: can you name a different downstream state for each of the four options, in a different phrase each time? If two share one, cut or merge — two options with the same consequence are one option wearing two coats.
- Sentence-length test: read the four options aloud — do at least two differ in length and shape? If all four scan identically, vary one.
- Announced-fails test: does an option name a virtue, a code, or a competence rather than enacting it? If yes, delete the naming and write the act alone.
- Trait-name test: can a reader state the trait the option scores for using only the option's own words? If yes, the option is answering the quiz for them — rewrite it as the act alone, and let the axis do the scoring.
- Want-at-risk handback: does the prompt stop before the outcome, with the wanted thing still in doubt? If the situation has already resolved and the options only react to it, cut earlier.
- Cost-free-yes test: before writing an option, name what it costs the taker in one phrase — if you cannot, that option is the obviously-correct one and the question is dead.
- Displacement test: does an abstract noun (loyalty, resentment, duty, integrity, ambition, betrayal) appear in the prompt? If so the answer key is in the question — displace it onto the concrete situation and delete the noun.
- Present-situation test: is this a situation happening now, or a biography of the taker? A résumé of attributes ("you have always been the one who…") fails; the taker arrives through a present situation and a want.
- One-telling-particular test: does the prompt carry exactly one load-bearing detail (the six years, the sixteen hours), or a catalogue? Keep the one that carries a judgment; cut the rest.
- Filter-word sweep: search the prompt and options for "you see / you notice / you feel / you realise" — delete the filter and present the thing directly.
- Intensifier test: strip every intensifier and adjective of degree (really, deeply, utterly, absolutely, very) — if the situation weakens, it was never loaded; find a harder particular instead.
- Second-person test: would the prompt read identically in third person? Then "you" is decorative — rebuild it around what the taker is doing or about to do.
- Capitals test: delete every capitalised word from the stem. If the dilemma still parses, it's diegetic. If it collapses, it was referential — the choice depended on knowing what the deleted word named.
- Substitution test: swap each Star Wars noun in the stem for its plain real-world equivalent. If the question becomes an ordinary question with nothing lost, it was costumed — add a world-rule until it can't be swapped back without the dilemma changing.
- Gloss-in-clause: at most one novel noun per question, and it is defined by the sentence it lives in ("the shift supervisor," not "the moisture farm, which is a kind of homestead"). A second undefined novel noun is a trivia risk, not flavor.
- Legibility test: could a reader who has never seen the source material answer this unit truthfully, and does the answer feel earned rather than costumed onto a generic dilemma? Fandom trivia and a mundane dilemma in a Star Wars costume fail this the same way.

## Standing rules
- **World budget goes in the stem, not the options.** A proper noun in an option makes the four compare on affinity with the source material instead of on behaviour — the taker picks the option that sounds coolest, not the one they'd actually do. Options stay in plain English; register-bearing nouns belong to the situation, not the choice.
- **Register beats nouns.** A working stem can be built entirely from register with zero named things: *manifest, requisition, the garrison, your papers, the shift supervisor, salvage rights, the part is three days out, nobody's answering the comm.* None of those is a proper noun; all of them are unmistakably this world.
- **Second person is situation, never role, never power, never side.** "You" is a person a long way from home with somewhere to be — true of every character on the roster, contradictory of none. Do not grant the taker a ship, a squadron, a lightsaber, a side, or an intuitive power ("you feel the Force," "you sense") — any of those collapses the result space, because a Force-sensitive taker makes Han, Leia, and Cassian all incoherent results.

## Kill list
- Trivia: a stem or option with a correct answer outside the reader
- Referential: the capitals test collapses it — a named thing must be known to answer
- Costumed: the substitution test passes clean — a mundane dilemma with nouns swapped, nothing else changed
- Role-preemption: granting the taker a ship, a squadron, a weapon, a side, or a power ("you feel the Force," "you sense")
- A capitalised, non-sentence-initial word inside an option — the world budget belongs to the stem
- More than one novel noun per question, or a novel noun left ungrounded by its clause
- Trait names inside an option: leader, loyal, ruthless, cautious, rebel, ambitious, brave, principled, reckless, selfless
- Announced virtue, code, or competence ("I don't leave people behind — that's who I am")
- Abstract nouns in the prompt naming the question's own subject
- Hedges and modals in an option: try to, probably, maybe, perhaps, sort of, I guess, hopefully
- One option markedly longer than its three siblings
- The obviously-correct option; any option that costs the taker nothing
- Two options that land the taker in the same downstream state
- A prompt that glosses what the situation means about the taker
- Filter-word openers ("you notice that," "you feel")
- Intensifier-dressed stakes; world-by-adjective
- Biography before situation; the character sheet recited back at the taker
- Decorative second person
- A rhetorical template repeated across consecutive units ("What do you do?" ×12)
- More or fewer than four options

## Micro-examples
Before: "Your loyalty to the team is tested when your manager asks you to cover for a mistake."
After: "Your manager asks you to sign off on numbers you did not check."

Before: "You have always been a cautious, methodical person, and now you must decide."
After: "The plan is three days old and two of its assumptions died this morning."

Before: "- Be brave and confront them directly about it."
After: "- Say it to their face, in the meeting."

Before: "- Probably try to talk to someone about it first."
After: "- Ask the one person who'd tell me I'm wrong."

Before: "- Refuse." / "- Say no."
After: "- Refuse, in writing, with your name on it." / "- Leave. Not loudly. Just stop being available."

Before: "You've been at the emergency for sixteen hours, which is longer than anyone expected, and you're exhausted but you notice you're still there, which says something about you."
After: "Sixteen hours into the emergency. What's actually keeping you there?"

Before (costumed): "Your speeder breaks down on the way to the meeting." — a car-trouble dilemma with one noun swapped; substitute "car" back in and nothing is lost.
After (diegetic): "The part you need is three days out and nobody's answering the comm." — the dilemma is now generated by a rule of this world (distance is fatal, nobody is coming to help), not by the noun.

Before (referential): "Would you back Lando's play at the table?" — fails the capitals test; delete "Lando" and there is no dilemma left.
After (diegetic): "The one holding the manifest has sold you out before, and still books the best rates in the sector." — the same betrayal-and-need dilemma, answerable cold.
