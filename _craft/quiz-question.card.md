# Card — quiz-question
Applies to every unit in the bank — one 1–2 sentence second-person situation plus exactly four terse declarative options — run before the unit is committed to `questions.ts`.

**Doctrine inverted 2026-08-27.** The bank used to ban all fandom content from the
questions ("zero fandom — mundane-but-loaded situations only, all the source material
lives in the results"). That made the quiz read as a generic personality test with a skin,
which the research finding behind this card explains: the Pottermore Sorting Hat is
75–80% world-neutral in its actual questions, and the magic is in the wrapper, not the
nouns. In-world questions are now the standard. The words themselves live per pack in
`_craft/packs/<pack-id>/vocabulary.md`; `question-lint.py` builds the ALLOW / WARN / DENY
rules from that file. This card carries the judgment calls.

## Exemplars

Each pack keeps five units at `_craft/packs/<pack-id>/exemplars.md`. **They are the
highest-fidelity statement of that pack's target voice** — not illustrations of this card,
but the thing this card is trying to describe. **They outrank any prose description of the
register, including the tests below. When the card and an exemplar disagree, the exemplar
is right and the card needs work.**

**Cap: five per pack.** Sworn: a new unit that beats one is **swapped in for the weakest,
never appended**. A growing exemplars file is a style guide, read once; five units are
re-read every session. The cap is the feature, and it is per pack because `craft-gate.sh`
only ever injects one pack's set.

They are also the known-good fixture in prose form: `question-lint.py --selftest` runs each
pack's exemplars under that pack's own vocabulary and requires zero Tier-A FAILs. **A rule
that fires there is wrong; the unit is not.**

## Categories
Every unit lands in one of four. Only the last is the target.
- **Trivia** — has a correct answer outside the reader (a lore fact). Bad.
- **Referential** — no correct answer, but the reader can't form a preference without
  knowing what a named thing *is*. Bad.
- **Costumed** — a world-neutral dilemma with nouns found-and-replaced ("car" → "speeder").
  Worst of the four: it reads as fake, which is worse than reading as generic.
- **Diegetic** — set inside the world, every noun plain English or glossed in the clause
  it lives in, and the dilemma is generated by a rule that is only true in this world.
  This is the target.

**`skinned-question` does not measure this, and satisfying it is not progress.** The
advisory fires on a stem with no world vocabulary at all — it detects *too little world*,
which is the generic failure, not the costumed one. The two failures sit on opposite sides
of the target, and the cheapest way to clear the advisory is to drop an ALLOW-tier noun into
a mundane stem, which converts a generic unit into a costumed one: from a mild defect into
the worst of the four. This happened to a whole 34-unit bank on 2026-08-28 and every unit
had to be rewritten.

So: **clear `skinned-question` by changing what generates the dilemma, never by adding a
noun.** If a stem has no world vocabulary because nothing in the world is acting on the
taker, the fix is a different situation, not different words. And a unit that trips the
advisory while being genuinely generated by a world-pressure is fine — leave it; the
exemplars carry one deliberately.

## World-pressures
Every question should trace to one. They are the load-bearing facts of the setting —
deliver them through the situation, not through a name.

**They are per pack, and this card does not restate them.** They live in
`_craft/packs/<pack-id>/vocabulary.md` → `## world-pressures`, alongside that pack's
ALLOW / WARN / DENY tiers and its `role-preemption` phrases, and `craft-gate.sh` injects
the active pack's set verbatim every turn. A pressure list on this card would be one
world's, silently applied to every world — the same failure the vocabulary tiers had when
they were constants in `question-lint.py`. Format: `_craft/packs/_FORMAT.md`.

## Legibility — the scene, and the shape that carries it

**Added 2026-08-28 after the shipped harry-potter bank was read cold and found unanswerable
on one pass.** Every rule above was satisfied and the bank still failed, because none of them
checks the thing a taker actually needs first: *what is happening, and to whom.* Diegetic and
legible are different properties, and the bank had the first without the second.

### The two-part stem

A unit is a **scene** and an optional **handback**. `Question.text` carries the scene;
`Question.ask` carries the handback, rendered beneath it as a second, lighter line.

> **text:** The first-year two benches down has the flame up far too high, and his cauldron
> goes over — across the desk, across your notes, across his hands.
> **ask:** You were not watching. Why not?

The scene puts the taker in a room. The handback closes the loop, so the four options read as
a choice rather than as a form to fill in.

**The handback varies, always** — never the same line twice in the bank, never the same line
in consecutive units, and absent entirely on roughly a third of them. Sometimes a question
(*"What do you do?"*, *"Why not?"*, *"Where were you looking?"*), sometimes pressure with no
question in it (*"He has not turned round yet."*, *"Two seconds, maybe three."*), sometimes
nothing, because a scene that has already landed does not need to be asked twice.

**This is not the repeated template the kill list bans, and the distinction is load-bearing.**
That rule exists because `"What do you do?"` ×12 stops carrying information — the reader's eye
skips it, and it becomes visual noise between the stem and the options. A handback that is
different every time is doing the opposite: it is the last beat of the scene, and it is where
the timing lives. Uniform it, and the rule fires for real. **`ask` is linted as part of the
prompt** (`question-lint.py` concatenates the two), so nothing in it escapes the rules above.

### Four rules for the scene

- **Name an animate participant, on the page.** The first-year, the prefect, the boy who sat
  next to you, the thing that cleans the dormitory. **`Whatever [verb]s the [noun]` is banned
  outright** — *"Whatever keeps the gate…"*, *"Whatever keeps the common-room fire lit…"*,
  *"Whatever dropped it…"* ran six-plus times in the shipped bank, and each one makes the
  reader solve a riddle before reaching the choice. The construction was reached for to dodge
  a WARN-tier noun; the cost is the whole unit.
- **The inciting event is on the page.** If a reader cannot say what just happened, the stem
  is not finished. `the-ghost-on-the-staircase` — *"The ghost has told it the only way it can
  tell anything, and you are being asked whether to add the part it left out"* — never says
  what happened, and no amount of rereading recovers it. This is delete-the-sentence run in
  reverse: not *does every sentence earn its place*, but *is the one that matters there at all.*
- **Present tense, physical verbs.** Something goes over, cracks, catches, drops, lands. A
  stem built on states and abstractions (*"the sworn kind and not the said kind"*, *"what your
  family is"*) reads as a proposition to be parsed, not a moment to be in.
- **Setting quota: at most 12 of 34** units set around a document, book, file, form, register,
  roll or press. The rest belong in classrooms, corridors at night, greenhouses, the pitch, the
  lake, the forest edge, the Great Hall, the hospital wing, stairwells, the grounds in weather.
  The register is the easiest place to generate a dilemma, which is exactly why it needs a cap
  — the shipped bank ran near-total on paperwork and its own authoring-notes had already named
  the result: *"the bank read as a civil-service drama."*

## Tests
- Cold-read test: hand the unit to someone who has not seen it. Can they say what is happening, to whom, and what is at stake — on one read, without going back? This is the first test, and a unit that fails it fails regardless of what it passes below.
- Delete-the-sentence: remove any sentence of the prompt — does the remaining prompt still pose the same choice? If yes, that sentence advanced nothing; cut it.
- Downstream-state test: can you name a different downstream state for each of the four options, in a different phrase each time? If two share one, cut or merge — two options with the same consequence are one option wearing two coats.
- Sentence-length test: read the four options aloud — do at least two differ in length and shape? If all four scan identically, vary one.
- Announced-fails test: does an option name a virtue, a code, or a competence rather than enacting it? If yes, delete the naming and write the act alone.
- Trait-name test: can a reader state the trait the option scores for using only the option's own words? If yes, the option is answering the quiz for them — rewrite it as the act alone, and let the axis do the scoring.
- Want-at-risk handback: does the prompt stop before the outcome, with the wanted thing still in doubt? If the situation has already resolved and the options only react to it, cut earlier.
- Cost-free-yes test: before writing an option, name what it costs the taker in one phrase — if you cannot, that option is the obviously-correct one and the question is dead.
- Displacement test: does an abstract noun (loyalty, resentment, duty, integrity, ambition, betrayal) appear in the prompt? If so the answer key is in the question — displace it onto the concrete situation and delete the noun.
- Present-situation test: is this a situation happening now, or a biography of the taker? A résumé of attributes ("you have always been the one who…") fails; the taker arrives through a present situation and a want.
- One-telling-particular test: does the prompt carry exactly one load-bearing detail (the six years, the sixteen hours), or a catalogue? Keep the one that carries a judgment; cut the rest.
- Filter-word sweep: search the prompt and options for "you see / you notice / you feel / you realise" — delete the filter and present the thing directly.
- Intensifier test: strip every intensifier and adjective of degree (really, deeply, utterly, absolutely, very) — if the situation weakens, it was never loaded; find a harder particular instead.
- Second-person test: would the prompt read identically in third person? Then "you" is decorative — rebuild it around what the taker is doing or about to do.
- Capitals test: delete every capitalised word from the stem. If the dilemma still parses, it's diegetic. If it collapses, it was referential — the choice depended on knowing what the deleted word named.
- Substitution test: swap each world noun in the stem for its plain real-world equivalent. If the question becomes an ordinary question with nothing lost, it was costumed — add a world-rule until it can't be swapped back without the dilemma changing.
- Gloss-in-clause: at most one novel noun per question, and it is defined by the sentence it lives in ("the shift supervisor," not "the moisture farm, which is a kind of homestead"). A second undefined novel noun is a trivia risk, not flavor.
- Legibility test: could a reader who has never seen the source material answer this unit truthfully, and does the answer feel earned rather than costumed onto a generic dilemma? Fandom trivia and a mundane dilemma in the world's costume fail this the same way.
- Narrator-certification test: does a clause tell the taker what the situation means about them instead of stating what is happening? "it's clear that…", "it's genuinely not your business", "which says something about you" all score the answer in advance. Delete the clause and state the fact that forces the same conclusion — "Guards are leaning on someone in the market, and nobody there knows your name" beats "…and it's genuinely not your business."
- Stated-emotion test: does an option instruct a feeling rather than an act? "Feel it land" cannot be chosen, only agreed with; a visible reaction ("Wear it for a second") can be. A feeling named as a *consequence* is fine and exemplar-blessed — "Fly it broken. You'll feel every wrong part of that decision."
- Opener-repeat test: read the first two words of every option in the bank together. An opening repeating across more than two *units* is a tic ("Say nothing" ×6, "Do it" ×4 — found and cleared 2026-08-28). Repeats inside one unit are fine when they are the design: `three-weeks-in` runs "Say yes" ×3 so the options differ only in which basis you trust.

## Standing rules
- **World budget goes in the stem, not the options.** A proper noun in an option makes the four compare on affinity with the source material instead of on behaviour — the taker picks the option that sounds coolest, not the one they'd actually do. Options stay in plain English; register-bearing nouns belong to the situation, not the choice.
- **Register beats nouns.** A working stem can be built entirely from register with zero named things: *manifest, requisition, the garrison, your papers, the shift supervisor, salvage rights, the part is three days out, nobody's answering the comm.* None of those is a proper noun; all of them are unmistakably this world.
- **Second person is situation, never role, never power, never side.** "You" is a person a long way from home with somewhere to be — true of every character on the roster, contradictory of none. Do not grant the taker a role, a side, a possession, or an intuitive power — any of those collapses the result space, because a taker who already has one makes most of the roster incoherent as a result. Each pack lists its own collapsing phrases under `## role-preemption`.

## Not ported from story-loop, and why

`_craft/` is adapted from `christian93kg/story-loop/_craft_research/`. One of its eight through-lines is deliberately **not** carried across. Recorded so it is not re-derived:

- **"Causality, not chronology — beats join on therefore/but, never and then."** Its scope is *consecutive beats in a chapter*, not clauses in a sentence — the ship test reads "put therefore or but between consecutive beats." A unit is one beat; there is no next beat to join. Applied to the stem's internal `", and"` hinge it refutes the exemplars, all five of which use it, and `_exemplars.md` says a rule that fires there is wrong. The hinge is the house form: state the want, then the thing putting it at risk. Two related metrics were checked the same way and cleared — the two-sentence option shape runs 30% in the bank and 30% in the exemplars, and `", and"` options 21% against 20%. Checked and rejected 2026-08-28.

## Kill list
- Trivia: a stem or option with a correct answer outside the reader
- Referential: the capitals test collapses it — a named thing must be known to answer
- Costumed: the substitution test passes clean — a mundane dilemma with nouns swapped, nothing else changed
- Role-preemption: granting the taker a role, a side, a possession or a power that collapses the roster — the pack's literal phrases are in its `vocabulary.md`
- A capitalised, non-sentence-initial word inside an option — the world budget belongs to the stem
- More than one novel noun per question, or a novel noun left ungrounded by its clause
- Trait names inside an option: leader, loyal, ruthless, cautious, rebel, ambitious, brave, principled, reckless, selfless
- Announced virtue, code, or competence ("I don't leave people behind — that's who I am")
- Abstract nouns in the prompt naming the question's own subject
- Hedges and modals in an option: try to, probably, maybe, perhaps, sort of, I guess, hopefully
- One option markedly longer than its three siblings
- The obviously-correct option; any option that costs the taker nothing
- Two options that land the taker in the same downstream state
- A prompt that glosses what the situation means about the taker
- Filter-word openers ("you notice that," "you feel")
- Intensifier-dressed stakes; world-by-adjective
- Biography before situation; the character sheet recited back at the taker
- Decorative second person
- A rhetorical template repeated across consecutive units ("What do you do?" ×12). Scoped to *repetition*, not to the handback itself — a varied `ask` is the scene's last beat and is required (`## Legibility`); a uniform one is this rule.
- An indefinite-referent stem opener — `Whatever [verb]s the [noun]`, `The one who [verb]s the [noun]` — where a plain participant would do
- A stem with no inciting event: the reader cannot say what just happened
- An option opening repeated across more than two units ("Say nothing…" ×6)
- A stem opener repeated as a shape across the bank ("[Duration] into X, and Y" ×5)
- A clause certifying what the situation means about the taker ("it's clear that…", "it's genuinely not your business")
- An option instructing a feeling instead of an act ("Feel it land")
- More or fewer than four options

## Micro-examples
Before: "Your loyalty to the team is tested when your manager asks you to cover for a mistake."
After: "Your manager asks you to sign off on numbers you did not check."

Before: "You have always been a cautious, methodical person, and now you must decide."
After: "The plan is three days old and two of its assumptions died this morning."

Before: "- Be brave and confront them directly about it."
After: "- Say it to their face, in the meeting."

Before: "- Probably try to talk to someone about it first."
After: "- Ask the one person who'd tell me I'm wrong."

Before: "- Refuse." / "- Say no."
After: "- Refuse, in writing, with your name on it." / "- Leave. Not loudly. Just stop being available."

Before: "You've been at the emergency for sixteen hours, which is longer than anyone expected, and you're exhausted but you notice you're still there, which says something about you."
After: "Sixteen hours into the emergency. What's actually keeping you there?"

Before (costumed): "Your speeder breaks down on the way to the meeting." — a car-trouble dilemma with one noun swapped; substitute "car" back in and nothing is lost.
After (diegetic): "The part you need is three days out and nobody's answering the comm." — the dilemma is now generated by a rule of this world (distance is fatal, nobody is coming to help), not by the noun.

Before (referential): "Would you back Lando's play at the table?" — fails the capitals test; delete "Lando" and there is no dilemma left.
After (diegetic): "The one holding the manifest has sold you out before, and still books the best rates in the sector." — the same betrayal-and-need dilemma, answerable cold.
